# Designing Cinematic Storytelling — Decision Reference

Use this reference after `skills/creative/cinematic-storytelling-workflow.md` has established the scene contract and beat sheet. It supplies decision rules, not a mandatory house style.

## 1. Viewer Knowledge and Point of View

The order of images controls meaning.

| Desired effect | Reveal order |
|---|---|
| Discovery with the focal character | character -> object/threat -> reaction |
| Suspense through superior audience knowledge | object/threat -> unaware character |
| Emotional identification | closer focal-character coverage, looks, reactions, POV or near-POV |
| Distance or objectivity | static wide view with environmental context |

The focal character is not necessarily the loudest or most active person. It is often the person who experiences, observes, or changes because of the event.

Before planning shots, answer:

1. Whose scene is this?
2. What does the audience know before the focal character?
3. What should they discover together?
4. What information must remain hidden?
5. What reaction completes the beat?

## 2. Shot Size and Relationship

| Story function | Default choice | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Geography, atmosphere, relative position | establishing, wide, or long shot | emotional distance; small details disappear |
| Body action, gesture, object handling | medium-wide or medium | unclear facial nuance |
| Thought, emotion, recognition | close-up | loss of geography when overused |
| Shock, terror, extreme detail | extreme close-up | melodrama or ambiguity without context |
| Relationship or threat in one space | two-shot, dirty single, OTS, connection shot | weak focal priority |
| Isolation or decisive reaction | clean single | severed spatial relation if not established first |

Reintroduce a wide or reset shot after a run of close-ups when the audience's spatial map may be fading.

## 3. Composition

- Give each shot one dominant center of interest. On a face, the eyes usually dominate.
- Use look space in the eyeline direction and walk space in the movement direction.
- Use short-siding only to create pressure, confrontation, or imbalance.
- Centered symmetry supports order, authority, ritual, or calm.
- Diagonals, odd groupings, and foreground/midground/background overlap support tension and depth.
- Use contrast, leading lines, and frames within frames to direct attention.
- Avoid joint cuts at the neck, elbow, wrist, knee, or ankle.
- Remove background hot spots, tangencies, head mergers, corner-leading diagonals, and low-contrast clutter.

## 4. Angle and Blocking

| Choice | Typical effect |
|---|---|
| Low angle | strength, authority, threat, scale; may simplify the background |
| High angle | vulnerability, isolation, powerlessness, objective geography |
| Frontal | strongest subjectivity and confrontation |
| Three-quarter | balanced subjectivity and dimensionality |
| Profile | graphic tension, opposition, observation |
| Rear three-quarter | connects a character to what they see |
| Canted frame | instability or psychological disturbance; use sparingly |

Blocking is choreography among performers, objects, camera, lens, and cuts. Design backward from the final required position: decide where the character and camera must end, then build earlier movement to arrive there naturally.

Use entrances, exits, crossings, turns, foreground wipes, and object handling to create motivated reframing and edit points. Naturalistic movement is not enough; movement must also preserve readable geography and useful coverage.

## 5. Beat and Coverage Design

Build the beat sheet from dialogue, action, nonverbal response, hands, looks, props, entrances, exits, and time changes.

For a conventional dialogue scene, a robust progression is:

1. wide master to establish geography and the action axis,
2. two-shot or OTS coverage to preserve relationship,
3. matching singles and close-ups as emotional pressure increases,
4. reactions and inserts for editorial control,
5. a reset wide when geography needs recovery.

Repeat complete actions across coverage to create overlap for matched-action editing. Break complex movement into smaller action units and manage the axis for each unit.

A long take can increase immersion, but it concentrates risk. When a failed moment would invalidate the scene, add fallback singles, reactions, inserts, or a simpler master.

## 6. Axis, Eyeline, and Screen Direction

- Define the action axis between characters or along dominant movement.
- Keep related angles on one side unless disorientation is the explicit story effect.
- In dialogue, opposing characters normally look in opposite screen directions.
- Matching eyelines in the same direction imply that characters share a third target.
- Preserve the character's screen position, camera height, movement direction, and OTS relationship across matched cuts.

Legitimate axis changes require a visible bridge:

- camera movement across the axis,
- a neutral frontal shot on the axis,
- an establishing reset,
- a cutaway or third-character POV,
- a stable common landmark,
- a foreground wipe that masks reorientation.

For AI generation, store a continuity lock for character appearance, wardrobe, props, handedness, time of day, eyeline, and screen direction. Carry the same wording into every shot prompt and regeneration.

## 7. Motivated Cuts and Shot Contrast

Cut when the viewer needs new information or a new angle serves the next beat better.

Strong cut motivations include:

- off-screen look or sound,
- an object, shadow, or person entering frame,
- reaction after impact or revelation,
- entrance, exit, door close, collision, splash, explosion, or light change,
- unstable composition created by movement,
- next-scene audio beginning early.

For a cut on action, place roughly the first third of the action in the outgoing shot and the remaining two thirds in the incoming shot. Adjust by rhythm and clarity rather than treating the ratio as mechanical law.

Adjacent shots must differ meaningfully in one or more dimensions:

- angle,
- shot size,
- subject position,
- movement direction,
- moving versus static camera,
- subject count,
- brightness, color, or texture,
- interior versus exterior,
- person versus object.

When matching continuous action, preserve screen position, eyeline, movement direction, camera height, and spatial relationship. Change the angle by roughly 30 degrees or create a substantial size change to avoid an accidental jump.

## 8. Camera Movement

| Need | Useful choice |
|---|---|
| Maintain framing on a moving subject | pan, tracking move, dolly |
| Share a look or head turn | POV pan or tilt |
| Intensify insight, recognition, or reaction | motivated push-in |
| Reveal isolation or environmental scale | pullback, optionally rising |
| Follow complex paths or walking dialogue | stabilized tracking / Steadicam grammar |
| Convey urgency, collision, crowd pressure | controlled handheld grammar |
| Add dimensional unease to static dialogue | slow lateral dolly or restrained orbit |

Movement should normally begin because action, attention, or drama demands it. Unmotivated movement can introduce a scene, reveal a clue, discover a witness, or withdraw emotionally; in the middle of a scene it often makes the camera feel self-conscious.

Zooms are conspicuous. Reserve them for surprise, rapid localization, snap emphasis, or a clearly stylized effect.

## 9. Lens and Focus

- Around 50 mm reads as comparatively natural.
- Wide lenses exaggerate depth, distance, approach speed, and spatial dimensionality. Avoid unattractive close-face distortion unless intended.
- Long lenses compress space and apparent movement, flatter faces, separate backgrounds, and can imply surveillance, dream, or danger. Handheld shake becomes more visible.
- Shallow depth of field isolates a subject and increases perceived importance.
- Deeper focus supports geography and simultaneous relationships.
- Rack focus transfers attention between subjects at different depths without cutting.
- Starting out of focus can express waking, mystery, temporal transition, dream, or tension when the reveal is motivated.

## 10. Transitions

| Relationship | Default treatment |
|---|---|
| Continuous time or parallel action | hard cut with strong shot contrast |
| Seconds to hours passing | establishing reset, time/weather change, exit/entrance, reveal, in-camera wipe |
| Large temporal or locational shift | explicit setting information, seasonal/event/technology change, inserted scene, fade |
| Memory, dream, substantial time passage | dissolve when the meaning is clear |
| Decisive interruption or shock | cut to black |
| paragraph ending, sleep, closure | fade to black |
| destruction, death, uncertainty, blast | fade to white when tonally justified |
| emotional or temporal bridge | J-cut / sound prelap |

Do not use a dissolve merely because two adjacent shots lack contrast. Fix the shot design first.

## 11. Storyboard and Shot-List Workflow

For every shot record:

- shot number and beat,
- purpose and viewer task,
- framing and angle,
- POV owner,
- lens character and focus behavior,
- character/object blocking,
- camera motivation and movement,
- axis side, eyeline, and screen direction,
- cut motivation and intended next shot,
- transition or sound bridge,
- continuity locks and risk notes.

Draw a scene map before detailed boards. Mark performers and movement with solid arrows; camera positions, direction, and movement with numbered dashed arrows. Mark the action axis and every intended axis bridge. Use the actual delivery aspect ratio. When the beginning and end of one shot have different framing, use connected panels.

Storyboard quality is measured by clarity of framing, action, camera movement, and edit intent, not illustration polish.

## 12. Review Checklist

### Before asset generation

- [ ] Focal character, conflict, emotional turn, and reveal order are explicit.
- [ ] Every beat has one viewer task and one dominant visual center.
- [ ] Geography, axis, eyelines, movement direction, and landmarks are locked.
- [ ] Master, relationship coverage, reactions, inserts, and fallback coverage are sufficient.
- [ ] Every shot has a purpose and intended cut motivation.
- [ ] Camera movement is motivated or tied to a named reveal/withdrawal function.
- [ ] Continuity wording is reusable across all generation prompts.

### Before edit approval

- [ ] Every cut improves the delivery of the next beat.
- [ ] Adjacent shots have enough contrast to avoid accidental jumps.
- [ ] Matched action preserves position, eyeline, direction, height, and spatial relation.
- [ ] Axis changes are visibly bridged.
- [ ] Wide and close coverage preserve both geography and emotion.
- [ ] Time and place changes are legible through shot design, transition, or sound.
- [ ] The scene remains understandable with sound removed.

## 13. Common Failure Patterns

| Failure | Correction |
|---|---|
| One wide shot covers every beat | Assign geography, relationship, emotion, reaction, and detail to different functional shots. |
| Focal character is unclear | Reassert POV ownership and give that character looks, reactions, and proximity. |
| Cuts feel arbitrary | Create demand through action, eyeline, sound, reaction, entry, exit, or reveal. |
| Similar adjacent shots jump | Increase angle, size, position, motion, light, or subject-count contrast. |
| Close-ups erase space | Reintroduce a master or reset wide. |
| Dialogue geography flips | Restore the same axis side or bridge the change visibly. |
| Camera moves without reason | Tie movement to subject action, attention, discovery, or emotional withdrawal. |
| Time compression feels like teleportation | Add exit/entrance, intermediate action, cutaway, wipe, or environmental change. |
| Long take failure collapses the scene | Add master, singles, reactions, and inserts as insurance. |
| Busy background weakens reverses | Cheat positions, simplify contrast, add depth, or use foreground occlusion. |
